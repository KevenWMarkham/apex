## The Story — Part V Walkthrough

### Chapter 29 — Qualifying

**KEVEN:** Chapter Twenty-Nine. Qualifying. And we previewed this in Episode One — the first-meeting qualifier, the red-flag / green-flag cheat sheet. Part Five formalises it.

The qualifying motion is — *forty-five minutes of structured conversation with a senior buyer.* Five questions, three red-flag categories, three green-flag categories, and a clear go / no-go at the end.

**Five questions** — what was your last AI-enabled change worth, what's your current data platform, who owns governance, who's the executive sponsor, and what does a successful Wave One look like to you. The Sellers Guide has the exact phrasing — section twenty-nine point two.

**Red flags** — buyer cannot name the sponsor, buyer's data is on three platforms with no consolidation plan, buyer recently failed a previous AI initiative without a written post-mortem. Any one of these — qualify out or restructure.

**Green flags** — buyer has a measured baseline, buyer has a named sponsor with authority, buyer has Microsoft as the strategic platform, buyer has a P&L outcome in mind. Two of four — proceed. Three of four — accelerate.

**JORDAN:** The single most under-used part of qualifying — the *written post-mortem* signal. Buyers who can walk you through why their last AI initiative didn't make it are dramatically easier to sell to than buyers who can't.

**KEVEN:** That's exactly right.

### Chapter 30 — Discovery and the Four-Week Workshop

**KEVEN:** Chapter Thirty. *Discovery and the Four-Week Workshop.* This is the chapter that defines how the BVA — Business Value Assessment — is structured.

The four-week workshop is the standard discovery motion. Week One — *baseline* — measure the current state, agree on the metrics, agree on the data sources. Week Two — *design* — pick the Wave One Service, design the agent orchestration, scope the data foundation. Week Three — *evidence* — build the minimum-viable agent pattern, demonstrate against real data, validate with the governance owner. Week Four — *commit* — present the Wave One scope, the commercial envelope, the joint roadmap.

**JORDAN:** And the output —

**KEVEN:** The output is *a Wave One scope the client's executive committee can approve.* Not a slide deck. A scope. With a number. With a calendar.

**JORDAN:** How often does the four-week workshop run shorter or longer than four weeks?

**KEVEN:** About 70% of pursuits run exactly four weeks. About 20% extend to five or six if the data foundation discovery surfaces unexpected complexity. About 10% compress to three if the buyer is highly aligned coming in. The Sellers Guide is very direct — *do not compress to two weeks.* Sellers who skip the evidence step lose deals at the executive committee.

### Chapter 31 — Proposal and Commercial Framing

**KEVEN:** Chapter Thirty-One. *Proposal and Commercial Framing.* This is the chapter that connects the BVA output to the actual SOW.

The framing principle — *the proposal is a story, not a price list.* Three sections — *the business case,* *the framework,* *the commercial envelope.* In that order. Never lead with the price.

**Business case** — what the Wave One Service does, what it delivers, what it measures. Two pages.

**Framework** — the APEX approach. The four pillars. The audit trail. The Independence posture. Two pages.

**Commercial envelope** — the price, the timeline, the assumptions, the dependencies. One page.

**JORDAN:** Five pages total.

**KEVEN:** Five pages total. The Sellers Guide is direct — *if your proposal is longer than ten pages, you have not done the discovery work.*

### Chapter 32 — Competitive Positioning

**KEVEN:** Chapter Thirty-Two. *Competitive Positioning.* The chapter we should all probably read once a quarter.

Three competitive postures.

**Posture One — "APEX versus AWS or GCP."** The argument is *not* technology comparison. The argument is *Microsoft stack maturity for governance.* Section thirty-two point — read it, but the line is *"Microsoft is the only hyperscaler with Fabric, Foundry, Copilot, and Purview as a composed governance-and-agent stack."*

**Posture Two — "APEX versus another consulting firm."** Don't bash. Differentiate on three things — the seven Practices (industry depth), the four-Microsoft-pillar composition (architectural precision), and the Independence posture (audit-ready evidence package). Section thirty-two point — three.

**Posture Three — "APEX versus the client's internal build."** This is the hardest. The line is — *"You can absolutely build this internally. The question is how many quarters of your platform team's time the framework saves you, and whether that's better spent on undifferentiated middleware or on the differentiated Use Cases that drive your P&L."*

**JORDAN:** When does internal-build actually win?

**KEVEN:** When the client has *world-class* internal data engineering and a multi-year platform investment already in motion. Then the right answer is sometimes — *let us do the Service work on top of your platform.* The Sellers Guide has the language for this — *"Deloitte as Service implementor, client as platform owner."*

### Chapter 33 — Objection Handling

**KEVEN:** Chapter Thirty-Three. *Objection Handling.* The most-referenced chapter in the guide. Thirty rehearsed objections, full verbatim responses.

We won't walk through them. Three categories.

**Category One — *price objections.*** "This is too expensive." Response framework — reframe to time-to-value, point to Wave One being smaller than the client's current AI spend, and offer phased commitment. Section thirty-three point two.

**Category Two — *platform objections.*** "We don't want to be locked into Microsoft." Response framework — *we are not arguing platform exclusivity, we are arguing that *for agentic AI* the Microsoft stack composition is the best available answer, and we co-exist with your other platforms via these interop patterns.* Section thirty-three point three.

**Category Three — *governance objections.*** "Our CCO isn't comfortable with AI." Response framework — *bring the CCO into Week One of the workshop. The four-week BVA is structured to give the CCO an evidence-package preview by Week Three.* Section thirty-three point four.

**JORDAN:** The metric to remember.

**KEVEN:** Thirty rehearsed objections. The Sellers Guide is direct — *if a seller faces an objection that isn't in this chapter, they should write it up and submit it to the Practice. The chapter grows.*

### Chapter 34 — Procurement and Close

**KEVEN:** Chapter Thirty-Four. *Procurement and Close.* And the procurement defense script we covered in Episode Two is the centerpiece. Section thirty-four point — the script, plus the verbatim language.

Three closing mechanics in this chapter.

**The legal-and-procurement timeline** — typically four to eight weeks for an enterprise client. Plan the close-out accordingly.

**The signature path** — who signs first, who approves second, who counter-signs third. Map it before the SOW lands in legal.

**The kickoff-week sequencing** — the moment the SOW signs, you have ten business days to spin up the delivery team. The Sellers Guide is specific.

### Chapter 35 — Delivery Handoff

**KEVEN:** Chapter Thirty-Five. *Delivery Handoff.* The chapter that prevents the most common Wave One failure — *the deal gets sold, but the delivery team doesn't know what was sold.*

The handoff is structured as a *forty-five-minute meeting* between the pursuit lead and the delivery lead, with a specific artefact — the *handoff brief.* The brief covers — scope, assumptions, dependencies, risks, the buyer's expectations, the commitments made in the proposal, and *anything the seller said that isn't in writing.* That last one is the one that breaks delivery if it's not captured.

**JORDAN:** The single biggest cause of Wave One delivery surprise is *something the seller said in a meeting that didn't make it into the SOW.* The handoff brief catches it.

### Chapter 36 — Microsoft Contact Center + Voice

**KEVEN:** Chapter Thirty-Six. *Microsoft Contact Center + Voice — Agentic AI in the Customer Service Channel.* This is a horizontal motion in the guide — it cuts across multiple Practices. Telco is the most natural fit, but RC, HLS, ER all have contact-center-adjacent workloads.

The pattern — *agent-assisted contact-center call.* The agent listens to the call in real-time, surfaces account context, recommends next-best-action to the human agent, drafts the post-call summary, emits the audit record. This is the *most demonstrable* APEX pattern, and section thirty-six point — has the pricing-and-staffing template.

**JORDAN:** When is contact-center the right Wave One Service?

**KEVEN:** When the client has a contact-center of at least 2,000 seats and a measurable handle-time or resolution-rate KPI they care about. Below 2,000 seats, the math doesn't pencil out at enterprise scale.

### Chapter 37 — Conclusion · The Seller Compact

**KEVEN:** Chapter Thirty-Seven. *The Conclusion.* The guide calls this *the Seller Compact.*

The compact is four commitments the seller makes to themselves and the client. I'm going to read them.

[reading]

*"One. I will qualify in or out within forty-five minutes. I will not waste a client's time pursuing a deal that won't transact."*

*"Two. I will compose the four Microsoft pillars in my own head before I walk into a client conversation. I will not confuse the client by treating them as four products."*

*"Three. I will hold Independence as a bright line. I will not take ECIF for client services. I will not promise what only the auditor can confirm. I will say 'audit-ready evidence package,' not 'audit.'"*

*"Four. I will hand off to delivery with discipline. Anything I said in a meeting that isn't in the SOW belongs in the handoff brief. Wave One does not surprise."*

[pause]

**JORDAN:** That's a good close.

**KEVEN:** That's the seller compact.

## The Series Wrap · Acquired-style

### Grading APEX

**JORDAN:** OK. Six and a half episodes in. Time to grade. Keven, I'm going to push on this. What does APEX get right, what does it get wrong, and what would you change?

**KEVEN:** Good. Let me grade three dimensions.

**Dimension one — the framework itself.** I give the framework an *A-minus.* The four-pillar composition is genuinely well-designed. The seven Practices cover the right industries. The canonical-schema kernel is the correct architectural choice. The minus is because there is still a real gap between the guidance for *how to compose* in the field and the *delivery patterns the firm actually executes* — meaning some Practices have richer playbooks than others. RC and AXLE are mature. ICE and TH are catching up.

**Dimension two — the commercial structure.** I give the commercial structure an *A.* The two-contract model, the wave envelope, the value-delivery chain, the procurement defense script, the funding paths — all of these are unusually well-thought-through for a consulting framework. The clarity protects sellers from making expensive mistakes.

**Dimension three — the Independence posture.** I give the Independence posture an *A-plus.* Most firms in this space pretend Independence isn't a binding constraint. APEX treats it as a design input. The result is a framework that is *actually deployable* at clients we audit — which is most of the Fortune 500.

**JORDAN:** Overall?

**KEVEN:** A-minus framework, A commercial structure, A-plus Independence. Average — *A.* I'd add — the framework has matured significantly in the last twelve months. The version of the Sellers Guide from a year ago would not have graded this well.

### Bull / Bear · the final pass

**JORDAN:** OK, final Bull and Bear. I'll go Bull first this time.

**Bull case.** APEX is positioned at exactly the right place in the market. Agentic AI is moving from experimental to operational. Buyers are no longer asking *if* — they're asking *how.* APEX is the only framework I've seen that gives a coherent answer across the four pillars, the seven industries, and the Independence constraint. If Deloitte and Microsoft execute the thirteen anchor account plan within four quarters, APEX is the *category-defining* framework for enterprise agentic AI. That's a strong bull case.

**Bear case.** Two real risks.

Risk one — *the framework's value is in the composition,* which requires *the seller* to compose. If the seller can't pitch four pillars in one conversation, the framework's value collapses to whichever pillar the seller leads with. Sellers are unevenly trained. The bear case is — *APEX is brilliant on paper, uneven in execution.*

Risk two — *the underlying platform is moving fast.* Microsoft Agent Framework. Fabric IQ. Foundry's new evaluation planes. Purview's new DSPM-for-AI. The guide has to keep up. The bear case is — *APEX as documented today is six months out of date by Q3 2026 unless updated continuously.* The Trilogy is a living document — but living documents are also the hardest to keep current.

**KEVEN:** Synthesis?

**JORDAN:** *APEX wins if Deloitte invests in two things — continuous seller enablement, and continuous content refresh.* The framework is the asset. The asset only compounds if you maintain it.

### Lessons — series-wide

**KEVEN:** OK. Series-wide lessons. Seven lessons. One per episode. Then we close.

**Lesson One — from Episode One.** *Canonical schema is the kernel.* If you take one thing from this series, it's that pre-built industry schemas — built on top of industry standards — are why APEX accelerates. Without that, you're not selling APEX. You're selling generic AI consulting.

**Lesson Two — from Episode Two.** *Two contracts. Three waves. Six envelopes. Three funding paths — never ECIF.* The commercial arc is the most disciplined commercial structure I've worked with in twenty-two years of consulting. Memorise the structure.

**Lesson Three — from Episode Three.** *Fabric is where the data lives. Foundry is where the agent thinks. Copilot is where the user types. Purview is what the auditor reads.* If you cannot deliver that sentence in eight seconds, you are not ready for the architecture conversation.

**Lesson Four — from Episode Four.** *Pick a Practice. Go deep.* RC and HLS for breadth. AXLE, ER, ICE for depth. TMT for technical chops. TH for narrative. One Practice. Two quarters. You're an expert.

**Lesson Five — from Episode Five.** *The twelve-section anchor account playbook is the most efficient pursuit prep document in consulting.* Use it for every anchor account. Adapt it for accounts that aren't on the anchor list.

**Lesson Six — from Episode Six.** *Same Practice. Same Service. Different framing.* The two airline anchors taught us that the same APEX asset wins different deals depending on the buyer's framing. Tailor the narrative. Don't tailor the asset.

**Lesson Seven — from this episode.** *Selling is something you do with the client, not to the client.* The four commitments of the Seller Compact are the operational summary of the entire series. Live by them.

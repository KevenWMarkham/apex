## The Story

### Practice One — RC · Retail and Consumer (Chapter 9)

**KEVEN:** RC. Retail and Consumer. The chapter starts with the question *why does RC matter* — section nine point one — and the answer is: RC is the largest Practice by revenue across Microsoft's industry portfolio, the buyer is unusually open to agentic AI, and the data foundations are usually weak enough that canonical schema is dramatic acceleration.

**Buyer.** Three personas — Chief Merchant, COO of Stores, Chief Digital Officer. The Chief Merchant cares about margin and assortment. The COO of Stores cares about labor and shrinkage. The CDO cares about customer experience and personalisation. Section nine point four has the full persona deep-dive.

**Three pressure points.** Section nine point two. Margin compression — gross margin is being squeezed by direct-to-consumer brands, by Amazon, by private label. Inventory volatility — supply chain unreliability is permanent now. Customer journey fragmentation — every customer crosses five channels and expects coherent service.

**Priority Service.** The Sellers Guide ranks them by deal frequency — and number one is *Store Operations Optimisation.* Labour, shrink, planogram, replenishment — all agentic. Number two is *Loyalty Churn Prediction and Winback.* Number three is *Returns Fraud Detection* — which sits in risk-fraud-security.

**Where APEX differentiates.** Section nine point six. Three places. One — canonical schema for orders, returns, inventory is pre-built on GS1 standards. Two — the Store 100 Reference Deployment shows a working store in production. Three — Purview classification for PCI and customer-PII is template-ready.

**Commercial envelope.** Wave One for a 400-store retailer — about 2.8 million dollars Deloitte services, 600K dollars annualised Microsoft consumption. ROI math we covered in Episode Two — 109 percent Year One.

**JORDAN:** Quick observation — RC is the *easiest* APEX sell in 2026. Because every RC CIO has tried something already and it didn't go to production.

**KEVEN:** That's exactly it.

### Practice Two — HLS · Healthcare and Life Sciences (Chapter 10)

**KEVEN:** HLS. Healthcare and Life Sciences. And HLS is interesting because it's actually three sub-segments inside one Practice — provider, payer, and pharma.

**Buyer.** Provider — CIO, Chief Medical Officer, Chief Quality Officer. Payer — CIO, Chief Medical Officer, Chief Risk Officer. Pharma — Chief Digital Officer, Chief Medical Officer, sometimes the heads of R&D and Commercial. Different buyers, different language, often different deals — pick one sub-segment per pursuit.

**Pressure points.** Provider — clinical workflow burden, prior authorisation, claims denial. Payer — utilisation management at scale, fraud-waste-abuse, member experience. Pharma — clinical trial recruitment, regulatory submission, commercial pricing. Section ten point one.

**Priority Services.** For provider — *Prior Authorisation Automation* — number one demand right now. *Clinical Decision Support* — high value, harder regulator path. For payer — *Claims Denial Prevention.* For pharma — *Clinical Trial Patient Matching.*

**Where APEX differentiates.** Section ten point four — *Purview's outsized role.* HLS is the Practice where governance and audit is *literally* the deal — without Purview, you don't get the conversation. APEX wraps HIPAA, HITECH, GxP audit requirements into the policy templates. We've also got the FHIR-aligned canonical schema anchored at Fabric Silver day one, feeding the per-Service Gold marts the agents consume.

**Commercial envelope.** HLS tends to land at the higher end — Wave One typically 3.5 to 5 million dollars Deloitte services. Microsoft consumption is also higher because of dedicated capacity for PHI workloads.

**JORDAN:** Honest observation — HLS is the *highest-trust* deal. Buyer cycles are longer. Once you win, you keep the relationship.

**KEVEN:** And the section to read for the pitch — section ten point nine — the *Day in the Charge Nurse's Shift* reference deployment. It's the story you tell.

### Practice Three — ER · Energy and Resources (Chapter 11)

**KEVEN:** ER. Energy and Resources. Three sub-segments — utilities, oil and gas, chemicals and materials. Each has a different regulator and a different operational pattern.

**Buyer.** Utilities — Chief Operating Officer, Chief Reliability Officer, Chief Customer Officer. Oil and Gas — Chief Operating Officer, Chief Technology Officer, Head of Subsurface. Chemicals — VP Operations, Chief Sustainability Officer, plant managers.

**Pressure points.** Energy transition — section eleven point two — every ER buyer is being squeezed by the transition. Utilities have grid modernisation pressure. Oil and gas has capital discipline pressure and the long-term demand question. Chemicals has the sustainability and circularity question.

**Priority Services.** *Distribution Outage Triage* for utilities — that's our flagship. *Predictive Wellhead Maintenance* for upstream oil and gas. *Refinery Yield Optimisation* for downstream. *Environmental Compliance Monitoring* across the segment.

**Where APEX differentiates.** Section eleven point four — *OT data and the Fabric edge.* ER lives on operational technology data — SCADA, historians, MES. Fabric has native ingestion patterns for this — Eventstream for the streaming, Mirrored DB for the historian replicas. We've also built the CIM canonical schema for utilities and OSDU for oil and gas. Standards are everything in this Practice.

**Commercial envelope.** Wave One typically 3 to 4.5 million dollars Deloitte services. Microsoft consumption is moderate — most ER workloads are batch-tolerant.

**JORDAN:** Section eleven point five — *safety as non-negotiable.* Every agentic decision in ER has to pass a safety gate. HITL by default. HOTL only after safety classification.

**KEVEN:** Yes. ER is the Practice where we are *most* conservative on the oversight spectrum. Always.

### Practice Four — AXLE · Automotive (Chapter 12)

**KEVEN:** AXLE. Automotive. And this is the Practice I know best — because the Zero Day Warranty scenario, which sits inside AXLE, is the worked example that opens about a third of our pursuits.

**Buyer.** Three buyer archetypes. The OEM CIO. The OEM Chief Quality Officer. And — increasingly — the head of Connected Vehicle. Section twelve point two.

**Pressure points.** Warranty cost. Quality escapes. Supplier risk. The collapse of build-to-field-to-supplier feedback into a single VIN-joined view. The transition to software-defined vehicles. And the EV / battery quality problem, which is its own sub-pressure-point.

**Priority Services.** *Warranty Traceability and Cost Avoidance* — the Zero Day Warranty scenario. *Predictive Maintenance — Stamping Press OEE.* *Quality Escape Detection and Root Cause.* *Connected Vehicle Diagnostics.*

**Where APEX differentiates.** Section twelve point four — *generative engineering as the wedge.* AXLE has the unique pattern where canonical schemas span four families — build record (the BRML family), connected vehicle (CVML), quality events (QEML), assembly telemetry (AAML). All four join on VIN. That four-domain join is the differentiator. No other framework has it pre-built.

**Commercial envelope.** AXLE deals are typically 3 to 6 million dollars Deloitte services for Wave One, often with a co-funding conversation involving NVIDIA — Omniverse, RAPIDS — for inline vision and high-throughput analytics.

**JORDAN:** And section twelve point nine — the AXLE framework versus the APEX AXLE Practice — *sibling frameworks*. Quickly?

**KEVEN:** AXLE is also a Deloitte-internal capability framework with its own depth — the Practice in APEX is the productised, Microsoft-platform-native subset. They are complementary. Sellers should know both names exist.

### Practice Five — TMT · Technology, Media, Telecom (Chapter 13)

**KEVEN:** TMT. Three sub-segments — TMT-TEC for technology firms, TMT-MED for media and entertainment, TMT-TEL for telecommunications. These are very different buyers.

**Buyer.** TEC — CIO, CTO, Chief Product Officer. MED — Chief Digital Officer, Chief Content Officer, Chief Revenue Officer. TEL — Chief Network Officer, Chief Operating Officer, Chief Customer Officer.

**Pressure points.** TEC — sophistication is high, the buyer asks hard questions, *section thirteen point two — sophistication raises the bar.* MED — content licensing, audience fragmentation, ad-tech disintermediation. TEL — contact-center transformation, network optimisation, fixed-vs-mobile convergence.

**Priority Services.** Section thirteen point three. For TEL — *Contact-Center Transformation* — biggest demand. For MED — *Content Rights and Royalty Reconciliation.* For TEC — *Developer Productivity and Code-Review Acceleration* — they buy the agent for themselves, which is fun.

**Where APEX differentiates.** Honestly — *TMT is the toughest Practice for differentiation,* because the buyers are technically sophisticated. APEX wins here on the *governance and audit story*, on the *Independence-of-recommendation story* — TMT buyers value the fact that we are recommending the Microsoft stack on the merits, not as a partner-channel motion — and on the *reference deployments*, section thirteen point eleven, the contact-center reference deployment.

**Commercial envelope.** Variable. TEL deals are typically 2.5 to 4 million dollars for Wave One. MED smaller. TEC highly variable.

**JORDAN:** Observation — TMT is the Practice where you should *ask the buyer* what they want to see in the pitch. They've seen everything. Don't pitch generically.

**KEVEN:** Right.

### Practice Six — TH · Travel and Hospitality (Chapter 14)

**KEVEN:** TH. Travel and Hospitality. Sub-segments — airlines, lodging, cruise lines, on-demand travel. The unifying buyer pressure is the *traveller profile* — section fourteen point three.

**Buyer.** Chief Commercial Officer for revenue management. Chief Customer Officer for experience. COO for operations — which in airlines means IROPs, in lodging means property management.

**Pressure points.** Revenue management volatility. Operational disruption — weather, mechanical, staffing. Loyalty competition — every major travel brand is fighting for share of wallet in the same five customers.

**Priority Services.** Section fourteen point two. For airlines — *IROPs Recovery Orchestration* — flagship. *Dynamic Rebooking.* For lodging — *Personalised Stay Experience.* Across all — *Revenue Management Augmentation.*

**Where APEX differentiates.** Section fourteen point three — *traveller profile as wedge.* APEX has a canonical traveller profile schema that unifies loyalty, transaction, preference, and operational data. Airlines and hotels typically have these in three different systems. Unification is the wedge.

**Commercial envelope.** TH Wave One typically 2.5 to 4 million dollars. Airlines tend higher than lodging.

**JORDAN:** Section fourteen point eight — *A Day in IROPs.* The airline reference deployment. The most fun read in the Sellers Guide, honestly.

**KEVEN:** [laughs] It is. The narrative is — a thunderstorm closes Atlanta, the agent has eight minutes to rebook 12,000 affected passengers. It's pacy.

### Practice Seven — ICE · Industrial Commercial Equipment (Chapter 15)

**KEVEN:** ICE. Industrial Commercial Equipment. Section fifteen point one — the scope is *industrial and commercial equipment manufacturers* — heavy machinery, construction, agriculture, material handling, industrial vehicles — and the aftermarket economics that sit downstream.

**Buyer.** Chief Operating Officer. Chief Aftermarket Officer — yes, that exists at several of the bigger players. Chief Customer Officer for warranty and service. Sometimes the head of the dealer network.

**Pressure points.** Section fifteen point two — *aftermarket economics.* These companies make their margin on accessories, service, repair, refurbishment — not on the initial unit. Optimising the aftermarket workflow is enormous money. Plus dealer-network complexity — section fifteen point four — three-tier and four-tier dealer structures with split-incentives across the chain.

**Priority Services.** *Warranty Claim Triage* — number one. *Service Parts Demand Forecasting.* *Dealer-Network Performance Analytics.* *Returns and Refurbishment Routing.*

**Where APEX differentiates.** Section fifteen point four — *dealer network complexity.* APEX has a canonical dealer-relationship schema that handles multi-tier dealer structures — the math is nasty in this segment and the schema is the wedge.

**Commercial envelope.** Wave One ICE typically 2.5 to 4.5 million dollars Deloitte services. Microsoft consumption moderate.

**JORDAN:** And ICE shares a lot of DNA with AXLE — both are manufacturing-plus-aftermarket Practices. Sellers cross-trained in AXLE can do ICE with a week of prep.

**KEVEN:** Yes.

### The cross-Practice observation

**JORDAN:** OK. We've done all seven in roughly twenty minutes. Cross-Practice observations?

**KEVEN:** Three.

One — **every Practice has a flagship Service.** Memorise it. RC is Store Ops. HLS is Prior Auth. ER is Outage Triage. AXLE is Warranty. TMT is Contact Center. TH is IROPs. ICE is Warranty-Claim Triage. When a seller knows the flagship by Practice, they enter the room with a thesis.

Two — **every Practice has a reference deployment.** Section nine point nine, ten point nine, eleven point nine, twelve point nine A, thirteen point eleven, fourteen point eight, fifteen point — wherever ICE has it. These are the narrative documents you read aloud in a discovery meeting. They land.

Three — **the seven Practices are not equal-weight.** RC and HLS are the highest-volume by deal count. AXLE and ER are the highest-dollar by deal size. TMT is the most technically demanding. TH and ICE are the most under-served, meaning the highest growth opportunity.

## Adopt / Hold

**KEVEN:** Adopt versus Hold. Today's twist — *which Practice should a new seller adopt first?* Jordan, you take Adopt.

**JORDAN:** Adopt — **RC and HLS.** Reasons. RC is the highest deal-count, easiest to land first-meeting on, biggest pool of accounts where Microsoft is already deployed. HLS is the highest-trust, longest-relationship Practice — once you win, you don't lose. A seller who specialises in RC plus HLS has the broadest sustainable pipeline. The Sellers Guide ranks RC and HLS as Tier-A in section nine point one and ten point one respectively.

**KEVEN:** Hold. When is *not* Adopt-RC-and-HLS the right call?

**JORDAN:** Three cases.

Case one — *if you have a deep industry background in one of the harder Practices.* If you're a former plant manager — go AXLE or ER. If you're a former telco network engineer — go TMT-TEL. The industry credibility outweighs the deal-count advantage.

Case two — *if your firm or your team has a competitive advantage in one of the niche Practices.* If Deloitte has just won three TH airline deals in your region — ride that wave.

Case three — *if you're chasing a specific named account.* Some named accounts are anchor accounts in specific Practices — Part Four of the guide. Match the Practice to the account, not the other way around.

**KEVEN:** Synthesis?

**JORDAN:** Default to RC and HLS for breadth. Deviate when you have a specific edge.

## Lessons

**KEVEN:** Monday-morning lessons.

One — **memorise the seven Practice names in order.** RC, HLS, ER, AXLE, TMT, TH, ICE. There will be a moment in a conversation when you need to list them. Be ready.

Two — **memorise the flagship Service per Practice.** One sentence each. You should be able to recite all seven in twenty seconds.

Three — **pick one Practice this quarter.** Read its chapter twice. Read the reference deployment three times. Spend an hour on the priority Service deep-dive in section X-point-nine of that chapter.

Four — **in every discovery meeting, identify which sub-segment you're in.** Provider vs payer vs pharma changes everything. Utility vs oil-and-gas changes everything. Don't merge them.

Five — **never describe a Practice in generic terms to its buyer.** They will spot it. Use the language from their industry — section X point two and X point four for personas and pressure points.

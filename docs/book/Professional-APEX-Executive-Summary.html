1 · The One-Sentence Version

APEX is a framework that accelerates the building of services and agents on the Microsoft Platform. Clients buy Services — productized envelopes with KPI commitments, sold to industry anchor accounts under a two-contract Independence model — and Deloitte's Microsoft practice builds those Services faster, more consistently, and with more rigorous governance by using the APEX framework. The current Service catalog runs to 38 productized Services across 7 industry practices, all built on APEX.

What gets bought · what does the building

The client buys a Service with a KPI envelope. The Service is built using the APEX framework. APEX itself is not productized for sale; it is the framework Deloitte's Microsoft practice uses internally to deliver Services on the Microsoft Platform. This distinction is structural, not stylistic — the two-contract Independence model (client ↔ Microsoft for platform, client ↔ Deloitte for the Service engagement) makes the Service the contractable unit, not the framework.

Why now

The reasoning runtime (Azure AI Foundry) and the data plane (Microsoft Fabric) are both production-ready in 2026. APEX is the framework that couples them under Independence-compatible Service-delivery patterns. Microsoft GTM teams want the Services; clients want the Services; APEX is what makes delivery teams able to ship them at the cadence the catalog implies.

4 · The Commercial Argument

First Service in a tenant is expensive; fortieth is cheap. The cost-per-decision drops two orders of magnitude as Services per tenant grow.

ScenarioServicesDecisions/dayTotal $/mo$/decision
Small (pilot)1~50~$1.4K$0.93
Mid (regional)3~3,000~$15K$0.06
Enterprise (global)8~80,000~$210K$0.011

The Layer 1 substrate amortizes across N Services in the same tenant. Adding the fourth Service to a Mid tenant changes Layer 1 cost trivially; only the Layer 2 marginal cost is added. This is what makes APEX a defensible commercial proposition: the architecture rewards land-and-expand.

5 · The Three-Year Maturation Arc

APEX is not a deploy-and-done system. It learns. The Sellers Guide commits a trajectory across three years that the Services Guide makes operational.

YearWhat the system hasWhat HITL looks like
1 · InstinctsHand-authored prompts · static rule librariesOperator approves most decisions
2 · RecordYear-1 LEDGER trains few-shot exemplars · per-persona personalizationOperator confirms most decisions; HITL is targeted exception-review
3 · SuperagentCross-Service generalization · Redis episodic memory · proactive anticipationOperator audits a sample; HITL is mostly confirmation; rare events get full attention
Why this is real, not aspirational

The maturation arc is enforced by two substrates that already exist: LEDGER (durable long-term memory · cryptographically anchored to Microsoft.KeyVault HSM) and Azure Cache for Redis Premium (hot working/episodic memory). Year-2 prompts are derived from Year-1 LEDGER. Year-3 prompts retrieve from Redis at runtime. The architecture is the curriculum.

6 · The Independence Posture

APEX is sold and deployed under a two-contract model: client ↔ Microsoft for the platform, client ↔ Deloitte for services, ECIF flowing direct from Microsoft. This is structural, not stylistic.

The architecture enforces Independence at four boundaries:

  • Two Container Registries · Deloitte authoring registry signs images; client tenant registry pulls verified images. Image bytes cross by mirror; no runtime path back.
  • Per-tenant Microsoft Fabric workspace · client SOR data and the LEDGER never leave the client's Microsoft platform tenancy.
  • Microsoft.KeyVault HSM CMK · client controls the cryptographic root; tokenization and HSM signing happen under client-owned keys.
  • Microsoft Purview audit graph · the client's Purview is the regulator-walkable audit substrate.

Cross-tenant learning is structurally forbidden (Services Guide ADR-025-01). Each client tenant is a closed superagent training universe. Federated learning is out of scope for v1 of the Trilogy.

8 · The Three Decisions a Leader Makes

Decision 1 · Sponsor a pilot Service for one client

  • Pick a client where the two-contract model is already understood (existing Microsoft platform footprint · Microsoft Fabric in flight).
  • Pick one Service from the catalog where the KPI envelope is measurable in 90 days (RC-E2E-03 · HLS-E2E-04 · TMT-TEL-RAA-04 are good first picks).
  • Pre-cost: ~$1,500/month Small-tier infrastructure · plus delivery hours for the first Service onboarding (~75 minutes per the Day-Zero motion in Volume III Ch 13–14).

Decision 2 · Authorize the trilogy as the delivery standard

  • Three volumes as the canonical reference for Sellers · Service Architects · Delivery Leads.
  • The trilogy's cross-volume reference index (Volume II Appendix I) is the citation discipline for derived artifacts.
  • Library landing page · single entry point at Professional-APEX-Library.html.

Decision 3 · Commit to the maturation arc

  • Year 1 is genuinely "instincts" — accept that Year-1 LEDGER is the curriculum for Year-2.
  • Don't pre-load prompts trained on synthetic or other-client data — that violates Independence (ADR-025-01).
  • Plan for Year-2 prompt refresh as a real engineering investment, not a "nice to have."

10 · One Last Thing

The book is finished. The architecture works. The MVP runs on a laptop in eight hours (Services Guide Ch 26). The Microsoft platform substrate is generally available. The 38-Service catalog has KPI envelopes anchored on real-world W2 pilots. The Independence posture is structural.

What's left is delivery. Pick a client; pick a Service; ship Year-1; let Year-1 LEDGER train Year-2; let Year-2 train Year-3. By the end of Year 3 the operator no longer asks "do I trust this recommendation?" — she asks "where did the system get this right last time?" and walks the lineage.

That moment is the commercial argument made operational.
